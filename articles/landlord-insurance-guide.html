Being a landlord comes with unique risks: a tenant could damage your property, a guest could sue for injury, or a fire could leave your rental uninhabitable. Standard homeowners insurance won't cover these scenarios—you need dedicated landlord insurance. This guide explains what landlord insurance covers, how it differs from other policies, and tips to get the best protection at an affordable price. Whether you own a single-family rental or a multiplex, understanding your coverage is crucial to safeguarding your investment.

What Is Landlord Insurance?

Landlord insurance is a specialized policy designed for residential or commercial properties you rent out to others. It typically covers the building structure, your liability as a property owner, and lost rental income if the property becomes uninhabitable due to a covered event. Unlike homeowners insurance, it assumes no one lives in the home full-time, so it accounts for the higher risks of tenant turnover, vacancy, and property damage.

Most standard landlord policies include three main coverage parts: property coverage for the dwelling and other structures (like a detached garage), liability protection for injuries or property damage that occur on the premises, and loss of rent reimbursement if a covered loss forces your tenant to move out. You can also add endorsements for things like vandalism, theft, or faulty workmanship.

Pro Tip: Always check if your policy covers “fair rental value” or “loss of rent” – this pays you the rent you would have collected while the property is being repaired. Some policies set a limit, like 20% of the dwelling coverage, so choose enough to cover your actual rental income.

What Does Landlord Insurance Cover?

Typical landlord insurance covers the following:

  • Dwelling Protection: Repairs or rebuilding of the rental structure from perils like fire, wind, hail, lightning, and vandalism.
  • Other Structures: Detached garages, sheds, fences, and patios.
  • Personal Property Used for the Rental: If you provide appliances (refrigerator, stove), furniture, lawn mowers, or snow blowers, these are covered.
  • Liability Protection: Covers legal fees and medical payments if a tenant or guest is injured on the property, or if you’re sued for property damage caused by your negligence.
  • Loss of Rental Income: Reimburses you for missed rent when the property cannot be rented due to a covered loss.

Many policies also include identity theft protection and coverage for building code upgrades if you need to rebuild to current standards. However, flood damage, earthquake, and normal wear and tear are usually excluded – you’ll need separate policies for those.

Landlord Insurance vs. Homeowners Insurance

Homeowners insurance is designed for owner-occupied homes, where the policyholder lives on the property. Landlord insurance is tailored for rental properties. Key differences include:

  • Occupancy Assumption: Homeowners policies assume the owner resides there, so they often exclude coverage for long-term tenants. Landlord policies are built for non-owner occupancy.
  • Personal Property Coverage: Homeowners covers the owner’s belongings; landlord policies typically exclude tenant personal property (tenants need renters insurance).
  • Loss of Rent: Landlord policies include loss of rent coverage; homeowners policies do not.
  • Liability Coverage: Landlord policies usually offer higher liability limits to cover slip-and-fall lawsuits and property damage caused by tenants.
  • Premium: Landlord insurance costs about 15–25% more than a comparable homeowners policy due to increased risk.

Using a homeowners policy for a rental is a risky mistake – it could void your coverage when you file a claim. Always disclose the property’s rental status to your insurer.

How Much Does Landlord Insurance Cost?

The average cost of landlord insurance varies widely based on several factors. Nationally, you can expect to pay between $1,000 and $4,000 per year for a single-family rental home, but this can be higher in disaster-prone areas or for high-value properties. Factors that influence your premium include:

  • Location: Properties in areas with high crime, wildfire risk, or frequent severe weather cost more.
  • Property Value and Rebuild Cost: Higher dwelling limits raise premiums.
  • Coverage Limits and Deductibles: Higher deductibles lower premiums; higher liability limits increase them.
  • Dwelling Age and Condition: Older properties may be riskier and cost more.
  • Number of Units: Multi-unit properties generally have higher premiums.
  • Your Claims History: A history of claims can increase rates.

How to Choose the Right Landlord Insurance Policy

Selecting the right policy involves balancing cost with adequate protection. Follow these steps:

  1. Evaluate Your Risks: Consider the property age, crime rate, natural hazards, and tenant types. For instance, short-term rentals (like Airbnb) may need different coverage than long-term leases.
  2. Determine Coverage Limits: Calculate the rebuilding cost of your property (not market value). Set liability limits high enough to protect your assets – at least $300,000 to $500,000.
  3. Decide on Deductible: A higher deductible (e.g., $2,500) lowers your premium but means more out-of-pocket costs for small claims. Choose one you can afford.
  4. Compare Quotes: Get at least three quotes from reputable insurance companies or independent agents.
  5. Check Exclusions: Read the fine print for exclusions like flood, earthquake, or damage from tenant negligence. Add endorsements or separate policies if needed.
  6. Review Financial Strength: Check the insurer’s ratings from A.M. Best or similar agencies to ensure they can pay claims.
Pro Tip: Consider a “landlord package policy” that bundles property, liability, and loss of rent, often at a discount. Also, require your tenants to have renters insurance to cover their belongings and reduce your liability risk.

Tips for Lowering Your Landlord Insurance Premium

Landlord insurance can be expensive, but there are ways to save without sacrificing coverage:

  • Bundle Policies: Insure your rental property and your personal auto or home with the same carrier for a multi-policy discount (often 10–20%).
  • Increase Deductible: Raising your deductible from $1,000 to $2,500 can reduce premiums by 10–15%.
  • Install Safety Features: Smoke detectors, fire extinguishers, security systems, and deadbolt locks can earn discounts. Some insurers also reward sprinkler systems or smart home devices.
  • Pay Annually: Paying the full premium upfront rather than monthly avoids installment fees.
  • Maintain Good Credit: Insurers often use credit-based insurance scores to set rates. Pay bills on time and reduce debt.
  • Review Policy Annually: As your property value changes or you add improvements, adjust coverage to avoid overpaying.

Additional Coverages to Consider

Standard landlord insurance leaves gaps that could cost you dearly. Consider these additional policies or endorsements:

  • Umbrella Insurance: Provides extra liability coverage above your policy limits (often $1 million or more). Essential if you own multiple rental properties or have significant personal assets.
  • Flood Insurance: Flood damage is excluded from most landlord policies. If your property is in a flood zone, a separate flood policy from the NFIP or private market is a must.
  • Earthquake Insurance: Similarly, earthquake damage is not covered. Consider this if you’re in a seismically active area.
  • Ordinance or Law Coverage: Pays for rebuilding to meet updated building codes, which can be costly.
  • Vandalism and Theft Coverage: Some policies limit this; ensure you have enough protection, especially for vacant properties.
  • Guaranteed Income Coverage: Some insurers offer a rider that pays a portion of rent even if the vacancy is due to a non-covered loss (like a tenant leaving early).

Evaluate your specific risks and talk to an insurance agent about customizing your coverage. For more on umbrella insurance, see our Umbrella Insurance Guide.

How to File a Landlord Insurance Claim

If your rental property suffers damage or a liability incident, follow these steps to file a claim smoothly:

  1. Ensure Safety First: If the property is unsafe, have tenants vacate and secure the premises. Notify your insurance company promptly.
  2. Document Everything: Take photos and videos of the damage, keep receipts for emergency repairs, and list all damaged items. This evidence is crucial.
  3. Contact Your Insurer: Call your insurance company or agent to report the claim. Provide your policy number, details of the incident, and any documentation.
  4. Mitigate Further Damage: Take reasonable steps to prevent further loss (e.g., cover broken windows, turn off water). Keep receipts for any expenses – they may be reimbursed.
  5. Work with the Adjuster: An adjuster will inspect the property. Be present if possible, and provide your documentation. Ask questions about coverage and settlement timelines.
  6. Review the Settlement: Once the adjuster submits a report, the insurer will offer a settlement. Review it carefully – if you disagree, you can appeal with additional evidence.
