How to Lower Your Homeowners Insurance Premium: 10 Smart Tips

Key Takeaways

  • Raising your deductible by $500–$1,000 can reduce your annual premium by up to 25%
  • Bundling home and auto insurance with the same carrier typically saves homeowners 10–20%
  • Installing security systems, smoke detectors, and deadbolts unlocks meaningful discounts with most insurers
  • Shopping around and comparing at least three quotes each year is one of the fastest ways to save
  • Reviewing your policy annually prevents you from paying for coverage you no longer need

Homeowners insurance is non-negotiable for most Americans — your mortgage lender requires it, and one severe storm or house fire can wipe out your life savings without it. But with the national average premium now exceeding $1,900 per year, many homeowners are paying far more than necessary. The good news is that a handful of strategic moves can shrink your bill by hundreds of dollars annually, often without reducing your core protections at all. In this guide, we break down 10 proven, actionable ways to lower your homeowners insurance premium starting today.

Why Your Homeowners Insurance Premium May Be Too High

Before you can lower your homeowners insurance premium, it helps to understand what is driving it up in the first place. Insurers use a complex mix of factors to calculate your rate, and many homeowners are unaware of all the variables at play.

Common Reasons Premiums Run High

  • Location risk: If your home sits in a flood zone, hurricane corridor, wildfire-prone area, or high-crime ZIP code, your insurer views it as a greater liability — and charges accordingly.
  • Home age and condition: Older homes with outdated plumbing, electrical wiring, or roofing materials are statistically more likely to produce claims, which raises your rate.
  • Claims history: Filing multiple claims in recent years — even small ones — signals elevated risk to insurers and can push your premium significantly higher for three to five years.
  • Low credit score: In most states, insurers use a credit-based insurance score to predict the likelihood of a future claim. A poor score can add hundreds of dollars per year to your premium.
  • Coverage mismatches: Many homeowners unknowingly insure their home for its full market value — land included — rather than its true replacement cost, or they carry far more personal property coverage than they actually own.

The good news is that most of these factors are either manageable or correctable. The strategies below address each one directly, giving you a clear, actionable roadmap to a lower premium without leaving your home or finances exposed.

Raise Your Deductible to Cut Your Premium

Your deductible is the amount you pay out of pocket before your insurance kicks in on a claim. It is one of the most direct levers you have for controlling your annual premium — yet most homeowners leave it sitting at the lender-required minimum of $500 or $1,000 and never revisit it.

How Much Can You Save?

The savings are substantial. According to the Insurance Information Institute, raising your deductible from $500 to $1,000 can cut your annual premium by up to 25%. Increasing it to $2,500 can save as much as 30%. On a $2,000-per-year policy, that is up to $600 back in your pocket every single year — without changing a single line of your actual coverage.

Is a Higher Deductible Right for You?

A higher deductible makes the most sense when you meet these conditions:

  • You have an emergency fund that can comfortably cover the full deductible without financial hardship
  • You have a strong claims-free history and a low likelihood of needing to file a claim in the near term
  • You are committed to handling small repairs out of pocket rather than running claims through your insurer

A practical rule of thumb: only raise your deductible to an amount you could pay tomorrow without going into debt or depleting your savings. If that number is $1,500, set your deductible at $1,500 — not $2,500.

Pro Tip: Avoid filing small claims where the payout would barely exceed your deductible. A $900 claim on a $1,000 deductible nets you almost nothing — but it can trigger a surcharge that raises your premium for three or more years, costing you far more in the long run.

Bundle Your Home and Auto Insurance for a Big Discount

One of the easiest and fastest ways to lower your homeowners insurance premium is to purchase it from the same company that handles your auto insurance. This is known as a multi-policy or bundling discount, and virtually every major carrier in the US offers it.

How Much Can You Save by Bundling?

Bundling home and auto insurance typically saves homeowners between 10% and 20% on each individual policy. Some insurers go even higher — carriers like USAA, Amica, and Erie have been recognized for bundling discounts approaching 25% in certain states. On a combined annual spend of $4,000 for home and auto insurance, a modest 15% bundle discount saves you $600 per year automatically, with zero change to your coverage.

Other Policies Worth Bundling

The savings do not stop with auto insurance. Many insurers offer additional discounts when you consolidate multiple policies, including:

  • An umbrella liability policy (which also extends your liability protection significantly)
  • A boat, motorcycle, or recreational vehicle policy
  • A life insurance policy issued through the same company
  • A landlord or renters policy for a second property you own or rent

Before moving everything to one insurer, compare the total combined cost across scenarios. Occasionally two separate best-in-class policies from different carriers cost less than a bundled package — but in most cases, bundling wins on both price and convenience.

Pro Tip: When requesting a bundle quote, ask the agent directly: "What is my total annual cost if I combine home and auto on one policy?" Some representatives will not volunteer the bundled rate unless you specifically request it.

Boost Home Safety and Security to Unlock Discounts

Insurance pricing is fundamentally about risk. The safer your home is from theft, fire, and accidental damage, the less exposure your insurer carries — and many companies pass that reduced risk directly back to you as a discount on your premium. Strategic safety upgrades can pay for themselves many times over through lower annual bills.

Security Systems and Monitoring

A professionally monitored burglar alarm can reduce your homeowners premium by 2% to 15%, depending on the insurer and the system. Systems that also detect fire, carbon monoxide, and water leaks typically earn the largest discounts. Smart home devices such as video doorbells, smart door locks, and automatic water shut-off sensors are increasingly recognized by carriers as risk-reduction tools as well.

Fire Safety Upgrades

  • Smoke detectors: Working smoke detectors on every floor are required by most policies. Upgrading to hardwired, interconnected models can earn a small additional discount and dramatically improve your family's safety.
  • Fire extinguishers: Keeping a certified extinguisher in the kitchen and garage signals responsibility to your insurer and can support a discount claim.
  • Whole-home sprinkler systems: A residential fire sprinkler system can earn premium discounts of 5–15% and substantially reduces the severity of any fire that does occur.

Door and Lock Upgrades

  • Deadbolt locks on all exterior doors are recognized by most carriers for a small discount
  • Reinforced door frames, security bars on sliding glass doors, and motion-sensor exterior lighting add further layers of documented protection

Always call your insurer before investing in upgrades to confirm which improvements qualify for a discount and by how much. Each carrier maintains its own approved list, and you will need documentation of any installed system to submit for the reduction.

Make Your Home More Disaster-Resistant

Catastrophic weather events are responsible for the majority of homeowners insurance claims in the United States. Insurers know this well, and they actively reward homeowners who take measurable steps to reduce their home's vulnerability to storms, wind, hail, and other natural disasters.

Roof Upgrades

Your roof is one of the single biggest factors in your homeowners insurance rate. An aging or poorly rated roof dramatically increases your premium — and in some states, insurers will refuse to renew policies on roofs older than 15 to 20 years. Upgrading to impact-resistant roofing materials rated Class 3 or Class 4 can earn premium discounts of 20–30% in hail-prone states such as Texas, Colorado, and Kansas. A new roof also eliminates the aging-roof surcharge many carriers apply automatically.

Wind and Storm Hardening

If you live in a hurricane or severe storm zone, the following upgrades can yield meaningful premium reductions:

  • Hurricane shutters or impact-resistant windows: Required by some Florida carriers; earn discounts of 5–15% in most coastal markets
  • Roof-to-wall connections: Installing hurricane straps or clips strengthens the structural bond between your roof and wall framing, a major failure point in high winds
  • Reinforced garage doors: Garage doors are one of the most common structural failure points during high-wind events, and bracing them is a low-cost, high-impact upgrade

Seismic Retrofitting

If you live in an earthquake-prone region — particularly California, the Pacific Northwest, or parts of the Midwest — seismic retrofitting such as bolting your home to its foundation and bracing cripple walls can reduce earthquake insurance premiums and may support discounts on your standard homeowners policy as well.

Pro Tip: Many states and local governments offer rebate programs for wind-mitigation and seismic upgrades. Check your state insurance department's website or visit the FEMA Hazard Mitigation Assistance page to find grants and rebates that can offset your upgrade costs before you spend a dollar.

Improve Your Credit Score to Pay Less on Your Premium

In 46 states plus the District of Columbia, insurance companies are legally permitted to use your credit history when calculating your homeowners insurance premium. They rely on a specialized metric called a credit-based insurance score — similar to your FICO score but weighted differently — to predict the statistical likelihood of a future claim. The result: homeowners with poor credit often pay dramatically more for identical coverage.

How Much Difference Does Credit Make?

The impact can be staggering. A homeowner with poor credit may pay 50–100% more than an otherwise identical homeowner with excellent credit for the same policy on the same home. That can easily translate to $500–$1,500 more per year in premium costs — money that compounds year after year if your score remains low.

Steps to Improve Your Insurance Score

  • Pay every bill on time: Payment history is the single largest factor in both your FICO score and your credit-based insurance score. Setting up autopay for all recurring bills is the single easiest protective step you can take.
  • Reduce credit card balances: Keeping your credit utilization below 30% — ideally below 10% — has a significant positive effect on your score over time.
  • Avoid opening multiple new accounts at once: Each hard inquiry temporarily lowers your score, and a cluster of new accounts signals financial instability to scoring models.
  • Dispute errors on your credit report: Visit AnnualCreditReport.com to retrieve your free reports from all three bureaus and dispute any inaccuracies in writing.

Credit improvements take time — generally 6 to 12 months before a meaningful change appears in your insurance score. But a jump from fair to good credit can produce a noticeable premium reduction at your very next renewal. Note: California, Massachusetts, and Maryland currently prohibit insurers from using credit scores to price homeowners insurance, so this factor will not apply if you reside in those states.

Review and Right-Size Your Coverage Every Year

One of the most common — and most expensive — homeowners insurance mistakes is paying for coverage that no longer fits your situation. An annual policy review takes less than an hour and can surface savings that have been hiding in plain sight for years.

Do Not Insure the Land

Your homeowners policy covers the structure of your home, not the land beneath it. Yet many homeowners set their dwelling coverage equal to the property's full market value — which includes land that can never burn down or blow away. If your home would cost $350,000 to rebuild but the total property value is $500,000, you may be over-insuring your dwelling by $150,000. Ask your insurer to provide a replacement cost estimate based on current construction costs per square foot in your area.

Right-Size Personal Property Coverage

Standard policies typically include personal property coverage equal to 50–70% of your dwelling limit. If your belongings are not worth that much, you may be able to negotiate a lower limit and reduce your premium. Complete a home inventory using a free app like Encircle or simply photograph every room to estimate the realistic replacement value of your possessions.

Audit Your Riders and Add-Ons

  • Do you still need that scheduled jewelry rider for a piece you sold or gave away?
  • Is the home-based business rider still relevant if you now work in a corporate office?
  • Are you paying for identity theft coverage through your homeowners policy when it duplicates a benefit already included in your bank account?
Pro Tip: Use a free coverage comparison tool like CoverageFixPro.com to audit your current homeowners policy against current market rates and identify specific gaps or areas of over-coverage before your next renewal date. It takes just a few minutes and can reveal whether you are paying too much for coverage you do not actually need.

Shop Around and Compare Quotes Every Year

Loyalty to your current insurer may be costing you money. Unlike some financial products where long-term relationships reward customers with better rates, homeowners insurance premiums are driven primarily by risk models — and those models do not reward years of paid premiums without a claim as generously as many homeowners assume. The only reliable way to know if you are overpaying is to compare.

How to Get the Best Possible Quotes

  1. Gather your current policy details first: Note your dwelling coverage amount, deductible, liability limits, and all riders. You need to compare identical coverage levels across insurers to get a meaningful comparison.
  2. Get at least three quotes: Contact your state's largest carriers directly and also speak with at least one independent insurance agent who can shop multiple companies simultaneously on your behalf.
  3. Compare total value, not just price: A lower premium that comes with a significantly higher deductible, weaker replacement cost provisions, or a financially unstable insurer is not a genuine improvement.
  4. Check insurer financial strength and satisfaction ratings: AM Best and J.D. Power publish annual ratings on insurer financial stability and customer claims satisfaction — check both before switching.
  5. Ask explicitly about every available discount: New-home discounts, claims-free discounts, retiree discounts, and first-responder credits are often not volunteered unless you ask directly.

When Is the Best Time to Shop?

Start comparing quotes 30 to 45 days before your current policy renews. This window gives you enough time to evaluate options, ask follow-up questions, and make a clean switch without any gap in coverage. Most policies can be canceled mid-term if you find a better deal, though some carriers charge a small flat cancellation fee. Homeowners who make a habit of shopping annually consistently report savings of $300–$700 per year — for roughly one hour of effort.
