You've built a successful business by providing expert services to your clients. But what happens when a client claims your advice caused them financial harm? Even the smallest oversight can lead to a costly lawsuit. Professional liability insurance, also known as errors and omissions (E&O) insurance, is designed to protect you in these situations. In this comprehensive guide, we'll explain everything you need to know about professional liability insurance: what it covers, who needs it, how much it costs, and how to choose the right policy for your business.

What Is Professional Liability Insurance?

Professional liability insurance is a type of business insurance that protects professionals against claims of negligence, errors, omissions, or failure to perform professional duties. If a client sues you for financial loss due to your professional advice or services, this policy covers legal defense costs, settlements, and judgments—up to your policy limit.

Unlike general liability insurance, which covers bodily injury and property damage, professional liability focuses on financial harm caused by your professional services. It's often required by contracts or licensing boards for many professions, including doctors, lawyers, accountants, architects, and consultants.

Pro Tip: Even if you're a sole proprietor working from home, don't assume your homeowner's or general liability policy covers professional mistakes. Most exclude professional services.

Who Needs Professional Liability Insurance?

Any business or individual that provides professional advice, expertise, or services should consider professional liability insurance. Common professions include:

  • Healthcare providers (doctors, nurses, therapists)
  • Legal professionals (lawyers, paralegals)
  • Financial professionals (accountants, financial advisors, tax preparers)
  • Technology consultants (IT consultants, software developers, web designers)
  • Real estate agents and appraisers
  • Architects and engineers
  • Management consultants and marketing professionals

Even if your profession isn't traditionally considered high-risk, if you offer advice or services for a fee, you could be sued for a mistake. Many clients today are quick to litigate, making professional liability insurance a smart investment for protecting your personal and business assets.

If you work on a contract basis, review your contracts carefully. Many clients require you to carry a minimum amount of professional liability coverage. Without it, you could lose the contract or be personally liable for claims.

What Does Professional Liability Insurance Cover?

Professional liability insurance typically covers claims related to:

  • Negligence: Failing to meet the standard of care in your profession
  • Errors and omissions: Mistakes or oversights in the services you provide
  • Misrepresentation: Incorrect or misleading advice
  • Breach of duty: Failing to fulfill professional obligations
  • Copyright infringement: Unintentional use of copyrighted material in your work product

The policy pays for legal defense costs, settlements, and judgments, even if the lawsuit is groundless. Most policies are written on a claims-made basis, meaning they cover claims made during the policy period, regardless of when the error occurred. It's important to maintain continuous coverage and consider purchasing a tail policy if you retire or switch carriers.

Pro Tip: Some policies include coverage for disciplinary proceedings by your licensing board. Check your policy or ask your agent if this is included, as legal costs for regulatory hearings can add up quickly.

Professional Liability vs. General Liability Insurance

Many business owners confuse professional liability insurance with general liability insurance. While both are important, they cover different risks.

General liability insurance covers third-party claims of bodily injury and property damage. For example, if a client slips and falls in your office, or if you accidentally damage a client's property, general liability pays for medical bills or repairs. It also covers personal and advertising injury, such as libel or slander.

Professional liability insurance covers claims arising from your professional services, such as errors in advice, failure to deliver promised results, or negligence in your work. The two policies complement each other; most businesses need both.

For instance, a web designer might need general liability if they drop a computer on a client's foot, and professional liability if they build a site that crashes a client's e-commerce platform. To learn more about general liability, read our comprehensive guide to liability insurance.

How Much Does Professional Liability Insurance Cost?

The cost of professional liability insurance varies based on several factors:

  • Profession: High-risk professions like doctors or lawyers pay more than low-risk fields like writers or graphic designers.
  • Coverage limits: Higher limits result in higher premiums. Typical limits are $1 million per claim and $2 million aggregate.
  • Business size: More employees and higher revenue usually increase risk and cost.
  • Claims history: Past claims can significantly raise premiums.
  • Location: Some states have higher litigation rates, driving up costs.
  • Policy structure: Claims-made policies are generally cheaper upfront but require ongoing tail coverage.

On average, small businesses pay between $500 and $3,000 per year for $1 million in coverage. Freelancers and consultants often pay less, while medical professionals may pay tens of thousands. How to Choose the Right Policy

Selecting the right professional liability insurance policy involves several steps:

  1. Assess your risk: Review your services, client contracts, and industry standards to determine potential exposures.
  2. Understand policy limits: Choose a per-claim and aggregate limit that adequately protects your assets. Most experts recommend at least $1 million per claim.
  3. Check for tail coverage: If you switch insurers or retire, a tail policy extends coverage for claims after the policy ends. Some insurers offer a free tail upon retirement after a certain period.
  4. Compare quotes: Get quotes from at least three reputable insurers. Use an independent agent or online marketplace to compare rates.
  5. Read the fine print: Pay attention to exclusions, deductibles, and defense coverage. Some policies cover defense costs outside the limit, which is preferable.

Remember, the cheapest policy isn't always the best. Ensure the carrier has strong financial ratings and a good claims handling reputation.

Common Exclusions and Limitations

No insurance policy covers everything. Professional liability insurance typically excludes:

  • Intentional wrongdoing: Fraud, illegal acts, or knowing violations of the law are not covered.
  • Bodily injury and property damage: These fall under general liability.
  • Employment practices: Discrimination, harassment, or wrongful termination claims need separate employment practices liability insurance (EPLI).
  • Cyber liability: Data breaches or cyber attacks causing client financial loss may be excluded. Consider a separate cyber policy.
  • Prior known claims: Any claim or incident you were aware of before the policy started is excluded.

Make sure you understand these gaps and consider purchasing additional policies to cover them. For example, if you handle sensitive client data, you might need both professional liability and cyber insurance.

How to File a Claim

If a client threatens legal action or you become aware of an incident that could lead to a claim, follow these steps:

  1. Notify your insurer immediately: Most policies require prompt notification. Delaying could jeopardize coverage.
  2. Preserve evidence: Keep all communications, contracts, invoices, and work product related to the claim.
  3. Do not admit fault: Avoid making statements that could be used against you. Let your insurer's legal team handle communications.
  4. Cooperate fully: Provide your insurer with all requested information and attend any required meetings or hearings.
  5. Understand the process: Your insurer will assign a claims adjuster and possibly an attorney. They will evaluate the claim and decide whether to settle or defend.
